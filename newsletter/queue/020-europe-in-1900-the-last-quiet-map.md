Forummapping's map of Europe in 1900 is one of the simplest in the whole series. A handful of large blocks fill almost the entire continent: the Russian Empire across the east, the German Empire and Austria-Hungary in the centre, the Ottoman Empire still holding a band of the Balkans between Greece and Constantinople, and the United Kingdom, France and Spain in the west. Sweden and Norway share a single color. It all looks settled. Within a decade, almost every one of those blocks would be under strain, and within twenty-five years several of them would be gone.

Paris, that year, was in a mood to celebrate. The Exposition Universelle ran from 14 April to 12 November 1900 and drew more than fifty million visitors, showing off a giant Ferris wheel, a moving walkway and much else meant to sum up a century of progress. The second modern Olympic Games were folded into the fair and spread across months, from May to October, with no opening or closing ceremony at all. They were also the first Games in which women competed. Hélène de Pourtalès won in sailing, and the British tennis player Charlotte Cooper became the first woman to win an individual Olympic event. On 19 July, without any ceremony, the first line of the Paris Métro opened between Porte Maillot and Porte de Vincennes.

Elsewhere the mood was darker. On the evening of 29 July 1900, King Umberto I of Italy was shot dead in Monza by the anarchist Gaetano Bresci, two years after troops had bloodily put down food-price protests in Milan in what became known as the Bava Beccaris massacre. Britain was bogged down in the Second Boer War in South Africa, a conflict in which more than 100,000 Boer civilians were forced into camps and around 26,000 of them died of hunger and disease. Then, in January 1901, Queen Victoria died after a reign of 63 years and 216 days. Her nine children had married into royal and noble families across the continent, which is why she was called the grandmother of Europe, and many of the crowned heads ruling the states on this map were her relatives.

Look closely at the map, because the cracks are already there. Sweden-Norway appears as one block, but it was only a personal union under the Swedish king. In June 1905 Norway's parliament voted to dissolve it, and in the August referendum that followed, 368,208 Norwegians voted to confirm the break and just 184 voted against. Sweden recognised Norway as an independent kingdom on 26 October 1905. Finland sits inside the Russian Empire as an autonomous grand duchy, but under Nicholas II the imperial government was pushing Russification policies that steadily cut into Finnish self-government. After the Russian monarchy collapsed, the Finnish parliament approved a declaration of independence on 6 December 1917.

The Balkans are where the 1900 map was most provisional. Bulgaria appears as a country, but in law it was still a tributary principality of the Ottoman Empire, a status inherited from the Congress of Berlin in 1878. On 5 October 1908 Bulgaria declared full independence, and on the very same day Austria-Hungary announced that it was annexing Bosnia and Herzegovina, which it had occupied and run since 1878. The Bosnian Crisis that followed drew protests from all the great powers and from Serbia and Montenegro, and the resentment it left behind fed directly into the tensions of 1914. Montenegro, still a principality on this map, was proclaimed a kingdom by Nikola I in 1910. Crete, drawn apart from the Ottoman Empire, had been an autonomous state since 1898, nominally under the sultan but protected by the great powers. It joined Greece in practice in 1908 and formally in 1913, after the First Balkan War.

Jump a quarter of a century ahead and the 1925 map is barely recognisable. The Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires have all gone. Poland, Czechoslovakia, Finland, the three Baltic states and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes have appeared, and Turkey has become a republic with its new capital at Ankara. Between the two maps lies the First World War, and the 1900 map helps explain why it was so hard to contain. Almost every large state on it was an empire ruling peoples who wanted states of their own, and the great powers were bound together by alliances and family ties that could turn a regional crisis into a continental one.

That is what makes the 1900 map worth a second look. It captures the last moment when the old order still looked permanent, drawn in a year when Paris was busy celebrating progress and few of the people at the fair could have pictured how much of it would be swept away within a generation.
