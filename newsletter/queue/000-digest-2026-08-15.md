Five stories from the past few days where the real subject was geography. Everything below comes from the wires — BBC World, The Guardian, Al Jazeera, Deutsche Welle and The New York Times, reported between 10 and 13 August — with the map added.

**1. Two grain exporters are dismantling each other's docks.**

Ukrainian strikes hit major Russian grain export terminals during an attack on a Black Sea port, the BBC reports, noting that exports from *both* countries are now falling as each attacks the other's shipping facilities. The NYT frames the wider picture as a Russian blockade of Ukraine's main ports that threatens global grain supplies, part of a long-running maritime battle. Al Jazeera reports a Russian strike sparking a fire at a Ukrainian port, with Zelenskyy again asking for interceptors — Ukraine has reported an acute shortage of Patriots since the US went to war with Iran in February. The Black Sea has one exit, the Bosphorus. Two of the world's largest wheat suppliers are both upstream of it.

**2. The strait stays shut, and Baghdad can't make payroll.**

Deutsche Welle reports that Iraq cannot export enough oil to fund its enormous public sector because of the Hormuz blockade, and that protests have already begun. In a separate piece DW reports Saudi Arabia has redirected exports away from the strait, while most Gulf producers still lack a reliable backup to the world's most important energy chokepoint. Al Jazeera's live coverage has Washington claiming "total control" of Hormuz and Tehran insisting its demands be met — flatly opposing claims about the same 33-kilometre gap. The Guardian reports UK GDP growth slowing to 0.4% in the second quarter, from 0.6%, as the ONS registers the war's drag. Geography here is destiny with a pipeline map attached: Saudi Arabia can cross its own peninsula to the Red Sea, Iraq's southern terminals cannot.

**3. A siege at Qusra, house by house.**

Israeli settlers have besieged Palestinian families in their homes in the occupied West Bank village of Qusra since Sunday, cutting off water and electricity and blocking medical care, the Guardian reports, describing an attempt to seize the properties. The BBC says Israeli security forces have reportedly withdrawn, leaving about a dozen settlers at the site. Accounts differ on scale: BBC, Guardian and NYT report two families besieged, Al Jazeera three. The NYT casts it as a spotlight on the army's struggle to curtail settler violence. Qusra sits south of Nablus, in the zone Oslo left under Israeli security control — the map's fine print, being redrawn one household at a time.

**4. Zambia votes, and the copper is still where it always was.**

Polls opened Thursday in Zambia's presidential election, Al Jazeera reports, testing whether President Hakainde Hichilema's economic reforms outweigh widespread frustration. The Guardian writes that the vote is haunted by his late arch-rival Edgar Lungu, whom he beat by a landslide in 2021 and whose family remains in feud with him after Lungu's death in 2025. The NYT reports Hichilema — elected promising to protect civil liberties in a copper-rich country — now stands accused of trampling them; DW lists youth turnout, women's representation and judicial independence among the issues. Counting was under way as our feeds closed. Landlocked and bordering eight states, Zambia's copper still has to leave by somebody else's coast.

**5. Putin lands on islands Japan says are Japan's.**

Japan has protested a visit by Vladimir Putin to the disputed Kuril islands — his first, according to Russian state media. This one is reported in our feeds by the BBC alone, so treat the detail as single-sourced. The four southernmost islands were taken by Soviet forces in the closing weeks of the Second World War; Japan calls them the Northern Territories and has never signed a peace treaty with Moscow because of them. It is the only unresolved land dispute left over from 1945 between two major powers, and a head of state stepping ashore is, in territorial terms, an argument.

**The data behind it**

Our Our World in Data map of oil production in 2024 explains the second story better than any narrative can — but only if you read it against a second map you have to supply yourself. The darkest countries are the United States, Russia and Saudi Arabia, with Iran and Iraq a band below. Production is spread across three continents. Now overlay the exits. American and Russian output reaches buyers by pipeline, rail and open ocean on two or three coastlines each. Almost everything in that dark cluster around the Gulf — Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, the UAE, much of Saudi Arabia's own volume — has historically left through a single 33-kilometre gap. The map of who has oil has barely moved in decades. The map of who can ship it changed in February.
