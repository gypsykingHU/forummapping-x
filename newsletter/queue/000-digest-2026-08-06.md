Five stories from the past few days where geography, not politics, was the real subject. Everything below comes from the wires — BBC World, The Guardian, Al Jazeera, Deutsche Welle and The New York Times — with the map added.

**1. Fifty thousand people cross a border that sits on the wrong continent.**

Around 50,000 Moroccans surged into Ceuta last Thursday, the NYT reports; most had returned within a day, and Spanish authorities put the toll among those attempting the crossing at at least 67 dead. Ceuta's leader, Juan Jesús Vivas, told the Guardian the influx was "if not orchestrated, at least encouraged or permitted" by Rabat. Spain installed a floating barrier; Al Jazeera reports hundreds of unaccompanied minors still stranded without food or shelter. Ursula von der Leyen has written to Pedro Sánchez urging work across five areas of migration control (BBC, Guardian), and Deutsche Welle reports the Commission calling the frontier a "shared European responsibility" while offering Morocco more support at both Ceuta and Melilla. Ceuta is 18.5 sq km of Spain on the African coast — one of two Spanish cities that give the EU its only land borders with Africa. NYT reports far-right leaders travelling there to demand expulsions. Twelve kilometres of fence line are now setting the tone for a continent.

**2. Iran talks to Oman, and a strait stays shut.**

Trump said fresh negotiations were Iran's "last chance" and claimed the Strait of Hormuz could reopen "by tomorrow," per the Guardian and Al Jazeera. Iran said it is talking to Oman about the shipping route but not to Washington (BBC, NYT). NYT reports Trump cancelled threatened strikes citing progress, with Saudi Arabia's leader urging him not to renew attacks. BBC reports the threat to tankers is now the worst since the war began, and the Guardian, citing Reuters, notes six Saudi-flagged supertankers rerouting in the Gulf of Aden. Hormuz is contested — and so is the detour around it.

**3. Europe's rivers stop working.**

The Rhine has fallen to record low levels, BBC reports, with the Rhine, Danube and Po all curtailing freight traffic and cutting electricity output. Deutsche Welle reports Hungary's Paks plant — the country's only nuclear station — ran at roughly 10% of capacity for lack of cooling water before shutting entirely, while Romania's military blew up a rock outcrop to improve flow to the Cernavoda plant and Serbia throttled two hydroelectric stations. The Guardian logged a new Austrian July record of 40.3C at Wieselburg. Three of Europe's great rivers are freight corridors, coolant supply and political boundary at once, and all three are low.

**4. The war's geography keeps widening.**

Russian officials say Ukrainian drone attacks on a Black Sea resort and annexed Crimea killed 11 — among them seven, three of them children, when debris struck a crowded beach (BBC, Guardian, NYT). NYT notes it was not clear what the drone was targeting, or why it came down there. BBC separately reports five dead in the Moscow region as Ukrainian strikes on warehouses continued, with facilities also hit in Tver and St Petersburg. These are Russian accounts and are not independently verified. Whatever the detail, the war's effective map now stretches from the Crimean coast to the Gulf of Finland, well over a thousand kilometres behind the front line.

**5. A country changes its name, and every atlas follows.**

Nauru is now the Republic of Naoero, the Guardian and NYT report, adopting the spelling and pronunciation used in the national language. Its international code shifts from NRU to NRO, and its people become dei-Naoero rather than Nauruan. The Guardian frames it as the latest move away from a colonial-era name. At about 21 sq km, Naoero is among the world's smallest states — a single raised coral island, strip-mined for phosphate for most of a century. It is also the sort of change that quietly propagates through every atlas, database and dropdown menu on earth.

**The data behind it**

Our World in Data's map of young people not in education, employment or training, 2024, explains story one better than any border map can. Morocco sits several shades darker than Spain — in a band it shares with much of North Africa, the Sahel and West Asia — while the northern Mediterranean shore is markedly lighter. NYT's reporting from Ceuta found precisely this: Moroccan migrants describing despair about their economic prospects rather than persecution. The steepest colour change anywhere near Europe on that map happens across fourteen kilometres of water. It is also, quietly, the same map behind the youth anger that BBC and Deutsche Welle both report is now confronting Narendra Modi in India. Data: Our World in Data (CC BY).
