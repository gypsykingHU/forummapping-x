Five stories from the past four days where geography is doing the talking: a Yemeni war reaching the Saudi capital, a ceasefire announced by one side of a front, a protest wave over India's voter rolls, a Pakistani province placed under federal control, and a deadly sea crossing between Africa and Arabia.

**1. Houthi missiles hit Riyadh's main airport.** Yemen's Iran-backed Houthis struck King Khalid International Airport on Saturday, killing 13 people, according to the BBC, the Guardian and the New York Times. The Guardian reports a further projectile hit a terminal on Sunday, killing one, and that Saudi Arabia extended its suspension of flights on Monday. Al Jazeera says the US, Australia, Canada and Japan are among countries warning citizens against travel to the kingdom, while the New York Times reports Iran has offered to mediate. The map matters because a war that long stayed along the Saudi-Yemeni frontier is now reaching the capital. The New York Times calls Saturday's strike the deadliest attack in a Gulf Arab country since the US-Israeli war with Iran began in February, and the BBC says Saudi Arabia's reputation for stability is taking a battering.

**2. Trump announces an "energy ceasefire" that Ukraine says it never heard of.** Deutsche Welle reports President Trump claimed Russia and Ukraine agreed to stop targeting refineries, power plants and other energy infrastructure. The Guardian says Zelenskyy called the deal "news to me", and that the Kremlin rejected a 60-day truce offer and declined to comment on the announcement. Al Jazeera reports Zelenskyy accused Russia of violating it. The New York Times adds that Trump said Ukraine should "get a new president". Al Jazeera adds that the truce has drawn criticism for easing pressure on Russia's war finances amid diesel shortages. Energy sites on both sides of the front line are the targets, so any pause, if it holds, would change which parts of the map are being hit.

**3. India's opposition marches on the Supreme Court over voter rolls.** Opposition leaders walked to India's top court on Monday demanding the election chief resign, the BBC and Al Jazeera report, after a weekend in which thousands of protesters were detained in Delhi. The Guardian says the anger centres on a revision of voter lists that removed 130 million names, and that Delhi was placed under severe lockdown. The New York Times reports leaders of the youth-led "Cockroach" movement were later released. The electoral roll decides who counts in each constituency, which is why a dispute over names becomes a dispute over the political map.

**4. Pakistan takes over Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.** The Guardian reports President Zardari declared an emergency and suspended the provincial government in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, run by Imran Khan's PTI, with hundreds of the party's politicians detained. The New York Times says the federal government cited a "severe breakdown in law and order" amid militant violence and named an allied official to run the province. Al Jazeera has an explainer on what emergency and governor rule involve. The Guardian adds that Khan supporters were marching toward Islamabad as the suspension took effect. Khyber Pakhtunkhwa borders Afghanistan, so control of it is both a domestic and a frontier question.

**5. More than 100 migrants die off Djibouti.** The BBC, citing the UN, reports 139 people died when a boat carrying 305 capsized near Obock on its way from Djibouti to Yemen. The New York Times, citing the International Organization for Migration, calls it among the deadliest wrecks on the route and says 15 children's bodies were recovered. The crossing runs through the Bab al-Mandeb strait, which Deutsche Welle notes lies close to the Houthi-Saudi fighting, so migrants are sailing toward a war zone.

**The data behind it**

This Our World in Data map shows oil production in 2024, in terawatt-hours. Three countries share the darkest band: Russia, the United States and Saudi Arabia. That is the link between this issue's first two stories: Saudi Arabia's airport and Russia's and Ukraine's energy sites are all part of the world's oil and fuel supply, which is why strikes on them are watched far beyond the region. Yemen, by contrast, is a pale sliver at the foot of the Arabian Peninsula, an oil-poor neighbour of the world's largest producers.
