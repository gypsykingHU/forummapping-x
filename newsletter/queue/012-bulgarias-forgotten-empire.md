Trace the borders of the First Bulgarian Empire at its widest, around 913, and the shape is startling: from the Carpathian Mountains to the Aegean Sea, from the Adriatic coast almost to the Black Sea, swallowing most of the Balkan Peninsula that the Byzantine Empire considered its own backyard. For nearly four centuries a state built by steppe horsemen sat astride the land bridge between Europe and Constantinople, and twice came close to taking the greatest city in Christendom outright. It is one of medieval Europe's least remembered superpowers, and the map explains why it worked when so many nomadic conquests didn't: this one built an administration to outlast its army.

The Bulgars began as outsiders to the Balkans entirely. They were a Turkic-speaking confederation from the Pontic steppe north of the Black Sea, part of the same nomadic world that produced the Huns and the Avars. Under pressure from the Khazars, one branch led by Khan Asparukh crossed the Danube around 680 and settled south of it, among Slavic tribes who had themselves arrived in the Balkans only a century or two earlier fleeing nobody in particular. Byzantium sent an army to evict them and lost. In 681, Constantinople did something it rarely did for a "barbarian" neighbor: it signed a treaty formally recognizing Bulgaria as a state and agreed to pay it tribute. A frontier had become a fact.

What followed was an unusual social arrangement. The Bulgars were a minority even in their own kingdom, a warrior aristocracy ruling a much larger Slavic peasant population. Within a couple of centuries the rulers had linguistically dissolved into the people they governed — the Bulgarian language that survives today is Slavic, not Turkic — while the state itself kept a Bulgar military structure and a Bulgar name. Few conquering elites in European history vanished so completely into their subjects while leaving their subjects the conquerors' name for a country.

The turning point was religious, and it came at the point of a sword. In 863, Byzantine forces exploited a famine and a string of earthquakes to corner Khan Boris I, and the price of peace was baptism. In early 864, Boris was secretly christened at his capital, Pliska, with the Byzantine emperor Michael III standing as his godfather. It was not really about doctrine. Boris needed one religion to bind Bulgars and Slavs into a single people instead of two, and Christianity, borrowed from the neighbor strong enough to have just beaten him in war, was the tool at hand. His own nobles saw it as a surrender of their steppe identity: in 866, fifty-two boyar families rose in revolt and were crushed and executed. The old order did not go quietly.

Boris then made a shrewder move than the conversion itself. Rather than let his new church be run permanently from Constantinople, he played Rome and Byzantium against each other for church jurisdiction and, in 870, secured formal autonomy for Bulgarian Christianity. A few years later he welcomed the exiled disciples of Cyril and Methodius, missionaries who had built a Slavic-language liturgy and alphabet for Great Moravia and been expelled after their patron's death. Bulgaria gave them a home, and their students at Boris's court in Preslav refined their script into what became the Cyrillic alphabet — arguably the single most consequential Bulgarian export in history, later carried to Serbia, Kievan Rus and beyond. A defeated khan's succession crisis produced the writing system of half of Slavic Europe.

His son Simeon I, who reigned from 893, took the empire to its peak and its most aggressive posture. A man educated partly in Constantinople, he turned his schooling to conquest, invading Byzantine territory repeatedly and besieging the imperial capital itself in 913 and again in 924. He styled himself Tsar — Caesar — of the Bulgarians, and for a moment extracted Byzantine recognition of an almost-equal imperial title, a claim no other Balkan ruler of the age dared make and live.

The empire that could threaten Constantinople could not survive its own success. Constant warfare drained Simeon's successors, and Byzantium waited for its opening. It came under the emperor Basil II, who fought a grinding, decades-long war of attrition and finally annexed Bulgaria outright in 1018, earning the nickname "Bulgar-Slayer." The First Bulgarian Empire's borders disappeared from the map for a century and a half, until a Second Bulgarian Empire rose in 1185. But the alphabet Boris had sheltered never went anywhere. Borders drawn by ninth-century wars turned out to be far less durable than the letters written inside them.
