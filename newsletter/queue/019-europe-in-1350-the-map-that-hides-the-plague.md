Look at Forummapping's map of Europe in 1350 and nothing on it announces a catastrophe. No empire has collapsed in a single campaign and no border has been redrawn overnight. The Holy Roman Empire sprawls across the centre, the Golden Horde fills the east, and France and England face each other across the Channel. Yet this is the map of the worst demographic disaster in European history. The plague that reached the continent in 1347 killed, by most modern estimates, somewhere between 30 and 60 percent of its people. The borders held. The people underneath them did not.

The plague came by sea. According to Wikipedia's account, it reached Europe in the summer of 1347 with Genoese traders fleeing Kaffa, a trading port in Crimea. Twelve Genoese galleys brought it to Sicily in October 1347, it reached Marseilles in late January 1348, and it arrived in England by June of that year. The map's biggest state sits right at the source. The Golden Horde, the Mongol-founded khanate that stretched from Siberia to the Danube, had reached its military peak under Özbeg, who ruled from 1313 to 1341 and made Islam the state religion. His successor Jani Beg tried to seize Kaffa, and the plague of the 1340s is described as causing a steep economic decline in the khanate.

The numbers are hard to hold in the head, and historians disagree on them. Ole Benedictow estimates that about 60 percent of Europeans died, around 50 million people. Other scholars suggest 45 to 50 percent. Pope Clement VI's agents counted 23,840,000 dead in Christian Europe in 1351, which works out to about 31 percent. City figures are just as stark. Paris is said to have lost half of its roughly 100,000 people. Florence fell from somewhere between 110,000 and 120,000 people in 1338 to about 50,000 in 1351. Hamburg and Bremen lost at least 60 percent. Whichever estimate you prefer, every region on the map was hit, and the plague kept returning: it was present somewhere in Europe nearly every year from 1346 to 1671.

War did not stop for it, though it did slow down. France and England had been fighting since 1337, and on this map they are still at it. The English won at Crécy in 1346 and captured Calais in 1347. Wikipedia describes the Hundred Years' War as an intermittent conflict that was frequently interrupted by external factors such as the Black Death, along with several years of truces. Fighting resumed, and the English won again at Poitiers on 19 September 1356. The conflict would eventually run for 116 years, ending in 1453.

In the centre of the map, the Holy Roman Empire was being reshaped by a ruler who built institutions. Charles IV was elected King of the Romans in 1346 and became King of Bohemia the same year. In 1348 he founded the University of Prague, the first university in Central Europe. He was crowned emperor in 1355, and his Golden Bull of 1356 set out how the imperial title would pass, a framework that lasted about four centuries.

To the east, Poland looks small beside Lithuania and the Golden Horde, but its king was doing something unusual. Casimir III, who reigned from 1333 to 1370, annexed Red Ruthenia, which is said to have doubled the size of his kingdom. He was also known for protecting Jewish communities, and that mattered a great deal in these years. The plague brought waves of persecution, as Jews were blamed for the disease. In Strasbourg in February 1349 about 2,000 Jews were killed, and the Jewish communities of Mainz and Cologne were destroyed in August of that year. By 1351, some 60 major and 150 smaller Jewish communities had been wiped out. Many survivors moved to Poland, where Casimir welcomed them.

In the Balkans the map shows a large teal Serbian Empire, and a Byzantine Empire reduced to scattered patches. Stefan Dušan was crowned Emperor of the Serbs and Greeks at Skopje on 16 April 1346, and in 1349 he proclaimed his law code, the first recorded code of Serbian public law. His realm reached from the Danube to the Gulf of Corinth. He died suddenly in December 1355 while planning a campaign against the Ottomans, and his son could not hold the empire together. It fragmented into independent Serbian states.

The plague changed more than the headcount. With fewer workers, wages rose and some rents collapsed, and landowners often switched from labour services to money rents to keep their tenants. The picture was mixed, since many labourers and craftsmen lost real income to inflation. That is the quiet lesson of the 1350 map. A snapshot of borders cannot show a society being reorganised from below, and the colors that look so stable are covering one of the great turning points of the Middle Ages.
