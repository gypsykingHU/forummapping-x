In 1931, the reborn Polish Republic counted its people and asked them what language they spoke at home. The results, mapped district by district, reveal a country with two faces. In the west and center, Polish dominated almost totally — above 90 percent nearly everywhere. But east of a rough diagonal line, the map changes color entirely: Ukrainian in the southeast, Belarusian in the northeast, Yiddish in the towns and cities everywhere. Roughly a third of the citizens of interwar Poland did not speak Polish as their first language.

The reason lies in how Poland came back to life. The state had been erased from the map in 1795, carved up between Prussia, Russia and Austria. When it was resurrected in 1918 out of the wreckage of three collapsed empires, its borders were not drawn along any clean ethnic line — because none existed. The eastern frontier in particular was settled by war: Piłsudski's armies pushed deep into the old Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth's historic lands, and the 1921 Treaty of Riga with Soviet Russia fixed a border far east of where most Polish-speakers lived. Poland gained the cities — Wilno and Lwów were largely Polish and Jewish — but the countryside around them spoke Ukrainian and Belarusian.

The 1931 census put numbers on it: of roughly 32 million citizens, about 69 percent declared Polish as their mother tongue. Ukrainian and the closely related "Ruthenian" categories accounted for around 14 percent, Yiddish and Hebrew about 8.5 percent, Belarusian over 3 percent, plus German and others. And the census likely undercounted the minorities — language, not nationality, was asked precisely because it produced a more flattering result for the state, and local officials had ways of nudging returns.

Governing this mosaic was the interwar republic's defining struggle. The constitution promised minority rights, and a 1935 international observer could find Ukrainian schools, Yiddish newspapers and Belarusian deputies in the Sejm. But the trend ran the other way: land settlement programs planted Polish veterans in eastern districts, Ukrainian-language schooling was steadily converted to bilingual or Polish, and the 1930 "pacification" of eastern Galicia — mass arrests and punitive raids after a wave of Ukrainian nationalist sabotage — poisoned relations for the rest of the republic's short life.

The Yiddish-speaking share of the map deserves its own paragraph, because it represented something found nowhere else in Europe. Interwar Warsaw was the largest Jewish city on the continent — over 350,000 people, roughly a third of the capital — and Poland as a whole held more than three million Jews, the heartland of Ashkenazi civilization. This was a complete parallel world in full flower: Yiddish daily newspapers with mass circulations, a celebrated literature, religious academies, socialist parties, sports clubs and film studios. On the 1931 map it appears as urban islands scattered across every region — a distribution, historians note, that made the community culturally powerful and politically defenseless at the same time.

The map's darkest historical weight, though, comes from what happened after 1939. The German-Soviet partition of Poland sliced almost exactly along the linguistic divide the census had recorded. The Holocaust destroyed the Yiddish-speaking third of the cities. Postwar border shifts moved Poland bodily westward — the eastern lands went to the Soviet Ukraine and Belarus, and millions of people were expelled or "repatriated" in both directions. The Poland that emerged in 1945 was, for the first time in its thousand-year history, nearly monoethnic. The 1931 language map is therefore a photograph of a vanished world: the last census of the old, multilingual Poland-in-between.
