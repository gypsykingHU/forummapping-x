This week the world's borders, chokepoints and frontiers did a lot of moving. From a European capital under fire to a Gulf shipping lane and a Saharan city changing hands, the lines that decide who controls what kept shifting. Here are the five stories that reshaped the map, and one from our library that rhymes with them.

**1. Russia hammers Kyiv as Ukraine takes the war to Moscow**
Overnight into July 2, Russia launched one of its largest missile-and-drone barrages on Kyiv in weeks, killing at least eight to ten people — children among them — and setting residential blocks and a hotel ablaze. The assault came hours after President Volodymyr Zelensky warned of a "massive" strike (BBC, The Guardian, Al Jazeera, NYT). Days earlier the war ran the other way: Russian authorities said they downed 419 drones across the country, including over the capital and Crimea (NYT). The static front line hides a widening war — both capitals are now targets, redrawing where "the front" actually sits. Analysts frame Kyiv's deep strikes as an effort to pressure President Vladimir Putin toward ending the war (NYT).

**2. The world's most important chokepoint is up for renegotiation**
After a short, violent US–Iran war, the Strait of Hormuz — the passage carrying roughly a fifth of the world's oil — has become the central bargaining chip. Trump said Iran agreed to indirect talks in Doha, though Qatar cautioned no direct US–Iran meetings were scheduled (BBC, The Guardian, Al Jazeera). Fresh strikes flared, with Iran reportedly hitting Bahrain and Kuwait as a ten-day-old memorandum frayed over conflicting readings (The Guardian). NYT reports Oman floated a plan for Iran and Oman to jointly charge passage fees — and that Iran is now rejecting the 1968 Iran–Oman pact governing the strait's lanes. Control of a few nautical miles is again reshaping Gulf power.

**3. Amnesty says ethnic cleansing accompanied the fall of el-Fasher**
Amnesty International concluded that Sudan's paramilitary Rapid Support Forces committed crimes against humanity and ethnic cleansing during their campaign to seize el-Fasher, the last major Darfur city held by the army (BBC, The Guardian, Deutsche Welle). The UN has said the assault bore the "hallmarks of genocide." El-Fasher's capture consolidates RSF control across Darfur, hardening a de facto partition of Sudan between the RSF-held west and the army-held center and east. A country that has lived within its 1956 borders for nearly 70 years is splitting along a line this war is drawing.

**4. Pakistan and the Taliban trade fire across the Durand Line**
Cross-border violence flared along the disputed Afghanistan–Pakistan frontier. Afghan officials said Pakistani airstrikes on three eastern provinces killed 36 civilians and wounded 163, which the Taliban called a "cowardly act of aggression" (The Guardian). Pakistan's military said Afghan Taliban forces launched strikes toward its territory and that it shot down four drones, vowing to respond to any further provocation (BBC). The two sides are contesting the Durand Line — the 1893 colonial-era border Kabul has never formally recognized. Four years after the Taliban's return, the frontier they inherited is once again a live front.

**5. Anti-immigrant protests push migrants out of South Africa**
More than 2,000 anti-foreigner protesters marched through Durban as an "arbitrary deadline" passed for undocumented migrants to leave, part of nationwide demonstrations that prompted authorities to deploy police, fearing a repeat of the 2008 xenophobic violence that killed 62 people (The Guardian). Nigeria's high commissioner said Abuja will seek compensation for businesses and property abandoned by citizens who have fled home (BBC). Migration is quietly redrawing southern Africa's human map: people who once crossed borders for work are being pushed back across them, reversing years of regional movement.

**The historical parallel**
Our map "Who Controls Afghanistan, 2021: April vs August" shows the country twice, months apart. In April the government (grey) still holds the cities and much of the north, with Taliban zones (blue) and contested districts (red) scattered between them. By August 12 the same outline is almost entirely red — the Taliban had swept the country in weeks, leaving only a few isolated government pockets. That collapse produced the very government now trading airstrikes with Pakistan across the Durand Line. The lesson the map makes visual: control of Afghan territory can flip faster than almost anywhere on Earth, and whoever ends up holding Kabul inherits a border dispute older than the state itself.
