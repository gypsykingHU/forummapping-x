Look at a map of Europe in 1453 and the first thing you notice is how much of the southeast belongs to the Ottoman Empire. The second is what's missing: the Byzantine Empire, the direct continuation of ancient Rome, has been squeezed down to little more than a single city and its walls. That city was Constantinople, and on 29 May 1453 it fell after a siege of 53 days. It is one of those dates that historians use as a marker between two ages, and the siege itself is worth reading closely, because the margin between holding out and collapse was thinner than the map suggests.

The attacker was Mehmed II, who became sultan in 1451 at the age of 19 and was about 21 when the city fell. He came with an army that modern historians put at somewhere between 50,000 and 80,000 men, including 5,000 to 10,000 Janissaries; contemporary Western chroniclers, frightened and prone to exaggeration, claimed figures of 160,000 to 300,000. Against that, the defenders could muster roughly 7,000 or 8,000 soldiers, a couple of thousand of them foreigners, and about 26 ships. Fewer than 50,000 people lived inside the walls when the siege began, swollen by refugees from the surrounding countryside.

The walls were the great equalizer. Constantinople's land defenses had held for centuries, and the Ottomans knew that ordinary methods would not be enough. So Mehmed relied on a Hungarian engineer named Orban, who cast an enormous bombard at Edirne: about 27 feet long, capable of throwing a stone ball weighing roughly 600 pounds more than a mile. It was terrifying but clumsy, taking around three hours to reload, and accounts differ on how long it lasted, with one tradition saying it collapsed after about six weeks. Even so, the sheer volume of gunpowder artillery trained on the walls marked a turning point in how cities were attacked and defended.

The defenders had two main hopes. The first was Giovanni Giustiniani, a Genoese soldier with a reputation for defending walled cities, who arrived in January 1453 with about 700 men and was given overall command of the land walls. The second was the harbor. The Golden Horn, the inlet on the city's northern side, was closed off by a chain stretched across its mouth and held afloat on logs, which kept the Ottoman fleet from sailing in and attacking the weaker sea-facing defenses.

On 22 April, Mehmed solved the chain. Rather than break it, he had his ships dragged overland on greased logs across the hill at Galata and launched them into the Golden Horn behind it. The defenders suddenly had to guard a long stretch of waterfront they had counted on being safe, with men they could not spare. It is the most famous maneuver of the siege, and a good reminder that the map lines of 1453 were drawn as much by engineering and improvisation as by armies marching.

The end came in the early hours of 29 May. A final Vespers service had been held in Hagia Sophia the evening before, with the city's few remaining defenders and citizens praying under its great dome. During the last assault Giustiniani was badly wounded and withdrew from the ramparts, and his departure caused panic among the defenders. The walls were overrun. Giustiniani himself died a few days later on the island of Chios. What happened to the last emperor, Constantine XI, is genuinely unknown: tradition says he threw off his imperial regalia and died fighting in the streets, but other accounts claim he hanged himself or was killed by marines while trying to reach the sea, and a head presented to Mehmed was claimed to be his.

What followed was brutal in the usual way. Looting was extensive, and about 30,000 of the city's people were enslaved. Mehmed reportedly lamented the ruin of what he had taken, saying, "What a city we have given over to plunder and destruction." Then he set about rebuilding. Constantinople replaced Adrianople as the Ottoman capital, Hagia Sophia was converted into a mosque, and Gennadius Scholarius was appointed Patriarch, so the Orthodox Church kept an institutional presence under Ottoman rule. In Rome, Pope Pius II called for a new crusade, but none ever materialized.

Now go back to the map. The color that spreads in 1453 is not just Ottoman territory but a new center of gravity: an empire that, with this city as its capital, would sit astride two continents for centuries. The countries around it, Hungary, Wallachia, Moldavia, the Serbian Despotate and Bosnia, are drawn on the map as separate states, and each of them would spend the following decades deciding how close to stand to the new neighbor. The fall of Constantinople did not redraw Europe overnight, but it moved the pivot on which the rest of the map would turn.
