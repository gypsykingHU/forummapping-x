Five stories from the past four days where the map is doing the talking: a Yemeni offensive aimed at the mouth of the Red Sea, a regional capital changing hands in Ethiopia, a separatist party on the edge of power in Quebec, a Brazilian race headed to a runoff, and a Bosnian election that redrew nothing.

**1. Yemen's government declares an all-out push against the Houthis.** Rashad al-Alimi, head of Yemen's Saudi-backed presidential council, announced military operations to retake all Houthi-held territory (BBC, Guardian). The Guardian reports the move came hours after the Houthis reportedly cut a vital supply route to the besieged city of Taiz. Al Jazeera says government forces claim to have taken Dhubab district and positions near the Bab al-Mandeb strait. The New York Times reports the Houthis claimed an attack on Saudi Aramco and that more than 200 US analysts are assisting Saudi Arabia. Dhubab matters because it overlooks one of the world's busiest shipping chokepoints.

**2. Ethiopian government forces take Mekelle.** Two residents of Mekelle, Tigray's regional capital, told the BBC that pro-government forces have taken control of the city after rebel forces withdrew. Deutsche Welle reports government advances against the northern rebel group and fears of regional spillover. The New York Times says the conflict risks drawing in Eritrea, Egypt, the UAE and Saudi Arabia. Last week's digest covered Tigrayan fighters pushing into Afar and Amhara; within days, the front has swung sharply back north to the heart of Tigray itself. Tigray borders Eritrea and Sudan, which is why every shift there is watched far beyond Ethiopia.

**3. Quebec votes with a separatist party in the lead.** Quebecers went to the polls on Monday with the Parti Québécois leading, according to the Guardian and the New York Times. The PQ has promised an independence referendum by 2028 (Guardian), three decades after Quebec voted "no" in 1995 (New York Times). If polls held, the Guardian notes, it would be the first time in more than a decade that the separatist movement has governed the province of roughly nine million. The New York Times cautions that voters want change, "but maybe not that much." Results were not yet available when our sources were compiled.

**4. Brazil heads to a runoff, with Flávio Bolsonaro ahead.** Flávio Bolsonaro, son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, finished first in Sunday's presidential vote but fell short of 50%, so he faces incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in a run-off on 25 October (BBC, Guardian, Al Jazeera, Deutsche Welle, New York Times). Lula called the result "unexpected" (Guardian), and Deutsche Welle describes Bolsonaro's lead as thin. The Guardian's morning briefing calls Bolsonaro the favourite for the runoff against the 80-year-old incumbent. Brazil covers nearly half of South America, so the outcome shapes policy on the Amazon and on the region's biggest economy.

**5. Bosnia's election runs along familiar ethnic lines.** Deutsche Welle reports that nationalists from the three main ethnic groups are set to dominate Bosnia-Herzegovina's three-member presidency, including Željka Cvijanović, who was backed by the disqualified Serb secessionist leader Milorad Dodik. Deutsche Welle also says new technology stopped votes being cast for dead or absent voters, but turnout fell to a record low and the same parties won. Bosnia's split into two entities, with a rotating Bosniak, Croat and Serb presidency, dates from the 1995 peace that ended its war. Both stories here come from Deutsche Welle alone.
