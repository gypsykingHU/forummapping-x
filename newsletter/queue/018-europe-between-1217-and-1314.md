Put two Forummapping maps side by side, Europe in 1217 and Europe in 1314, and the western half looks almost familiar. France, England, Norway, Sweden, Poland and Hungary are all still there. The eastern half is another matter. A vast green block labeled Kyivan Rus' has vanished, replaced by an enormous yellow one called the Golden Horde, and the Balkans and Anatolia have been reshuffled. Ninety-seven years is not long in medieval terms, but this was one of the most violent stretches of the Middle Ages, and the map records where it landed.

Start in 1217. Kyivan Rus' is shown as a single large state, though in reality it was a patchwork of principalities whose princes fought one another as often as they cooperated. In the southeast, the Fourth Crusade has left its mark. In 1204 the crusaders took Constantinople and set up the Latin Empire, which would last until 1261. The Byzantine Greeks did not disappear; they regrouped in successor states, and the 1217 map shows Nicaea and Epirus alongside the Latin Empire, each claiming to be the true heir of Rome. Bulgaria is strong, Serbia and Bosnia are small, and Hungary sits on the Danube as one of the major kingdoms of central Europe.

The west has its own turning points. In England, King John had sealed Magna Carta on 15 June 1215, only for the charter to be annulled soon after. In Iberia, the Christian kingdoms won the decisive Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa on 16 July 1212 against the Almohad Caliphate, whose territory still covers the south of the peninsula on the 1217 map. Within a few decades that southern block would shrink dramatically, and by 1314 only the small emirate of Granada remains in the far south, while Castile has grown into the largest Iberian kingdom.

Then came the Mongols. After campaigns through the Turkic and Caucasian lands, a Mongol army commanded by Batu Khan and planned by the general Subutai moved into eastern Europe. Kyiv fell in 1240. In April 1241 the invaders struck at both Poland and Hungary, winning the Battle of Legnica on 9 April and the Battle of Mohi on 11 April. Hungary was devastated. Yet the Mongols did not stay in central Europe; they withdrew, and the traditional explanation ties the withdrawal to the death of the Great Khan Ögedei, though historians debate how much that mattered compared with logistics and the difficulty of the terrain.

What they left behind was a new state. The Golden Horde became a functionally independent khanate after the Mongol Empire divided in 1259, and the territories it controlled in the east of Europe stretched far across the steppe. On the 1314 map it dominates the whole eastern third of the continent, drawn in yellow, with dotted lines marking the Russian principalities that now paid tribute to it. Its military power peaked under Özbeg Khan, who ruled from 1312 and adopted Islam, a shift that would change the religious map of the steppe. The old Kyivan state had not been wiped off the map so much as dismantled into pieces and made subordinate.

Next to the Horde, a second newcomer appears: Lithuania. In 1217 the Baltic coast is a scatter of small pagan tribes and the crusading Livonian Brothers of the Sword. By 1314 a Lithuanian state has taken shape, led by rulers such as Mindaugas, who was crowned king in 1251 and ruled until 1263. After his death the state survived and grew, and under Gediminas it started to expand into the Ruthenian lands. Vilnius became the capital in 1323, only a few years after the map's date. In 1386 the Union of Krewo would tie Lithuania to Poland in a partnership that shaped the region for centuries.

The Byzantine story took a more hopeful turn. On the 1217 map the Latin Empire occupies the heart of the old Roman east. On the 1314 map it is gone. In 1261 the Latin Empire was brought to an end and the Byzantines recovered Constantinople. They are back on the map, though much weakened, surrounded by a patchwork of small states in Anatolia that would eventually become the nucleus of something bigger, the Ottomans.

If you zoom out, the story is one of centers shifting. In 1217 the political weight of eastern Europe sat on the Kyiv-centered Rus' lands and the Byzantine rump states. By 1314 it had moved: toward Sarai on the Volga, toward Vilnius, and toward a Constantinople in Byzantine hands again. The map only shows the borders, but each border hides a decision, a battle or a death. Which of the changes between the two maps do you find most surprising?
