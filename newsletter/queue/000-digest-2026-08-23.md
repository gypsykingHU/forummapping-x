Five stories from the past few days in which the line being asserted wasn't a border at all — it was a tariff schedule, a shipping lane, a road network, an earthwork, a flight path. All of it from the wires — BBC World, The Guardian, Al Jazeera, Deutsche Welle, The New York Times, 18–22 August — with the map added.

**1. Tariffs arrive on the world's longest undefended border.**

A new 50% US levy on $20bn of Canadian imports came into force after a last-minute breakdown in trade talks, the BBC reports, with Prime Minister Mark Carney promising to match it "dollar for dollar." The Guardian has Carney accusing Washington of unfair eleventh-hour changes after a three-day extension; Al Jazeera and the NYT report the same collapse, the NYT noting Carney suspended talks and that both sets of tariffs now take effect. Two days earlier the NYT had a Canadian official still hopeful. The Guardian separately reports leaked audio of JD Vance mocking Carney for trying to "out-tough" Trump. The 8,891-km line between them was fixed by the conventions of 1818 and 1846 as a parallel of latitude, drawn deliberately to ignore terrain. Nothing about it is fortified. The customs schedule is now doing the work the map never asked it to.

**2. A strait declared "American territory."**

At a rally in South Carolina, Trump called the Strait of Hormuz "an American territory," the Guardian reports, as the US continues a military blockade of Iranian shipping and ports; Al Jazeera's live coverage carries the same line alongside his claim that Tehran is "not ready to make the right deal." Al Jazeera and Deutsche Welle both report Iran's president, Masoud Pezeshkian, saying it is time to end the war "from a position of strength." The NYT frames the threatened "economic D-Day" as pressure aimed less at Iran than at its trading partners, and reports Beijing's calculation that it can absorb the loss of Iranian crude; the BBC notes Iran's long practice at evading sanctions. On the map, Hormuz is not open water. The lanes run through Omani territorial sea, governed by transit-passage rules that belong to no navy in particular.

**3. Thirty families move into a settlement that had been closed.**

Israel has re-established a previously closed West Bank settlement, the BBC reports, with thirty "pioneer families" arriving and Palestinian residents nearby describing fear at the speed of the change; a companion BBC piece documents a significant expansion of building and road construction on occupied land. The Guardian reports settler attacks in Areas A and B — the zones under direct Palestinian rule and earmarked for a future state — have almost doubled since 2025. Al Jazeera and the NYT report a 17-year-old Palestinian shot dead and another person critically injured after settlers entered a village, with Palestinian and Israeli accounts diverging and the UN responding. Oslo II left Area C at roughly 60% of the territory. What the map cannot show is that the roads, not the zones, decide who reaches what.

**4. Poland is building an earthwork.**

Russian strikes killed two more Ukrainians a day after at least sixteen died in a double-tap drone attack on a shopping centre, the BBC reports. The Guardian places it in Kryvyi Rih, Volodymyr Zelenskyy's home town, with the EU's Kaja Kallas calling it "terror by design"; Al Jazeera reports the same strike, and the NYT attributes the intensifying barrages to Ukraine's depleted stock of interceptors. Against that, Deutsche Welle — alone in our feeds — reports that German army sappers have been helping Poland construct the "East Shield," a fortified barrier along its northern and eastern frontiers, since June. The BBC, Guardian and NYT all cover an underground weapons cache found in a forest near Berlin, with German investigators reportedly examining Russian links and seeking an extradition from Romania; the NYT notes officials named no culprit.

**5. Flights to countries the deportees have no connection to.**

Twenty deportees from the US arrived in Liberia, the Guardian reports, the first of as many as 1,200 under a new agreement and among the largest third-country deportations of the crackdown. The NYT reports the Liberian government has not clarified their status, and that at least five people resisted leaving the plane and were flown onward to Equatorial Guinea. Al Jazeera reports a federal judge in Manhattan striking down the State Department's suspension of immigrant visas affecting 75 countries. The geography is not incidental: Liberia was founded in 1822 as a settlement for freed African Americans, and its capital is named after a US president. It is the one country on the map built as a destination for people sent away from America.

**The historical parallel**

Set our map of Poland's shifting borders beside the fourth story. Four outlines on one frame: 1000, Bolesław the Brave's realm hugging the Oder and the Carpathians; 1596, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth stretching pale and enormous into what is now Belarus and Ukraine; 1939, the Second Republic, narrower, its eastern edge deep inside today's Lithuania and Ukraine; 1945, in red, the whole country slid west. The Commonwealth outline runs several hundred kilometres east of the modern one. The thousand-year-old outline sits almost exactly on top of it. Poland spent four centuries as the state without a defensible eastern edge, then had that edge moved for it twice in six years. The East Shield is the first time it has been fortified by choice.
