Kyiv counted its deadliest night in months, Iran staged a funeral across two countries, earthquakes redrew a stretch of Venezuela's coast, Sudan's war rolled east, and in Britain politicians began sketching the unthinkable. Here are five stories from the past few days that moved the map — and one map from our library that rhymes with the last of them.

**1. Russia's biggest strike on Kyiv — while its wider war stalls**
Russia hit Kyiv with what the BBC called the "most massive" attack on the capital yet, killing at least 30 people across a wide area of the city. President Vladimir Putin then visited the front and vowed to take more of Ukraine, dismissing Kyiv's recent gains as "imaginary" (NYT). Yet the same week, NYT analysts reported Russia's broader campaign has largely stalled, with Moscow suffering higher casualties than Ukraine — and that Crimea, the peninsula Russia annexed in 2014, is becoming a pressure point of drone strikes, fuel shortages and power cuts. In Warsaw, Prime Minister Donald Tusk warned Poland faces "critical months" ahead of a possible Russian threat (BBC).

**2. Iran buries Khamenei — with a funeral that crosses a border**
Iran began six days of public mourning for Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, killed in February during the US–Israeli strikes at the outset of the Iran war (BBC, NYT). Millions are expected in Tehran, where Deutsche Welle reports the leadership has turned the funeral into a deliberate show of power. The geography is the story: after lying in state, Khamenei's body will travel through five cities in Iran and Iraq, taking in some of Shia Islam's holiest sites (NYT) — a state funeral procession across an international border, tracing the religious map that underpins Iran's regional network. Meanwhile, DW reports the UN nuclear watchdog says inspections of Iran's nuclear sites are possible "in principle" — if Tehran allows real access.

**3. Venezuela's coast, redrawn by twin earthquakes**
The twin earthquakes that tore through Venezuela's northern coast have left overwhelmed morgues, families identifying victims in tents (BBC), and rescuers describing a "war zone" as international teams comb the rubble (The Guardian). Al Jazeera reports the quakes reduced a tourist town to rubble and its economy to tatters. Amid a soaring death toll came one moment of joy: a 44-year-old security guard pulled alive from a collapsed car park after eight days (BBC, NYT). This is the map changing by geology rather than politics — whole districts of the Caribbean coast will have to be rebuilt or abandoned, in a country already hollowed out by crisis.

**4. Sudan's war rolls east from Darfur toward the Nile**
Amnesty International says the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces committed crimes against humanity — including ethnic cleansing, murder, torture and enslavement — in their campaign to capture El Fasher, Darfur's last army-held capital (The Guardian). Now attention is shifting 700 kilometres east: a former UN humanitarian coordinator warns on Al Jazeera that the crisis at El Obeid "could be worse than El Fasher." El Obeid is the crossroads city of North Kordofan, the gateway between Darfur and Khartoum. If Darfur's fall was one front line collapsing, the fight for Kordofan is about whether Sudan effectively splits in two along a north–south line through its centre.

**5. Britain's neighbours start planning for the end of Britain**
The Guardian reports — alone among this week's outlets — that political leaders in Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland have begun quietly gaming the breakup of the United Kingdom in the event Reform UK wins the next election, or even becomes a strong opposition. Per the report, both unionists and nationalists are war-gaming the constitutional turmoil a Nigel Farage-led government might trigger. Nothing has changed on the ground, and Reform has won no such election. But when governments start contingency-planning for internal borders becoming international ones, the map is already moving in people's heads — which is usually where it moves first.

**The historical parallel**
A map from our library imagines exactly the scenario Story 5 tiptoes around — through the lens of Hungary's fate a century ago. The Treaty of Trianon (1920) stripped Hungary of roughly two-thirds of its territory after the First World War. Our map, "Trianon, if it happened to UK," applies that scale of loss to Britain: Scotland is handed to Norway, the north-east of England to Germany, the island of Ireland united under Dublin, and the south coast carved away for France — leaving a rump "UK" squeezed in the middle. It is a hypothetical, drawn to make Hungarians' historical shock legible to others. But it makes the point this week's Guardian report hints at: unions that look eternal on the map can be redrawn within a single political generation.
