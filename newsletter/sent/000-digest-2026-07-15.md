Five days, five stories that moved a border, a government, or a coastline — plus a map from our library that explains one of them.

**1. US and Iran trade strikes again as war reignites over the Strait of Hormuz**

The ceasefire from last month's Iran war unravelled this week: the US launched fresh strikes on Iranian targets, and Tehran hit American military facilities in Bahrain, Kuwait and Jordan (Al Jazeera, The Guardian). Iran also fired on a commercial ship and rattled shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway carrying roughly a fifth of the world's oil (NYT, Deutsche Welle). Brent crude jumped more than 4% on the news (Al Jazeera). The strait is one of geography's great chokepoints — barely 33km wide at its narrowest, pinched between Iran and Oman, and carrying tankers that supply much of Asia and Europe. Whoever can threaten to close it, even briefly, can move global energy prices without firing a shot at an oil field.

**2. Zelensky reshuffles his government as a WWII-era dispute threatens Ukraine's arms pipeline**

President Volodymyr Zelensky replaced prime minister Yuliia Svyrydenko in a wider leadership shake-up, which the NYT frames against a war that has recently "turned in Ukraine's favor" (Deutsche Welle, NYT). The reshuffle landed the same week Macron hosted Zelensky, Starmer and Merz in Paris for fresh "coalition of the willing" talks (The Guardian). But an old border wound resurfaced alongside it: Poland's PM pledged a memorial to victims of WWII-era killings by Ukrainian nationalists, and Warsaw is now weighing whether to cut Kyiv's arms aid over the dispute (BBC, Deutsche Welle) — a reminder that even allied borders carry old scars.

**3. Gibraltar's border with Spain opens after 118 years**

Gibraltar and Spain removed border controls between the British overseas territory and the Spanish mainland, ending more than a century of checkpoints (BBC — the only outlet in our feeds to report it). Officials hope free movement will boost the economy on both sides of a line that has been a flashpoint since Britain took Gibraltar under the 1713 Treaty of Utrecht, and which Spain periodically sealed, most notably during a 1969–1985 blockade under Franco. Sovereignty doesn't change, but one of Europe's smallest and oldest disputed frontiers just got softer.

**4. Typhoon Bavi forces evacuation of nearly two million in China**

Typhoon Bavi made landfall in eastern China's Zhejiang province after killing 17 people in the Philippines and skirting Japan and Taiwan; at its peak the storm was roughly as wide as France (NYT). Chinese authorities suspended schools, work and transport and evacuated close to two million people (BBC). Storms of this scale don't redraw borders, but they do reshape a coastline's human geography for weeks — mass evacuations, halted transit, and flooding that can put low-lying settlements out of commission long after the wind dies down.

**5. Venezuela's earthquake becomes a political earthquake**

Three weeks after twin quakes devastated Venezuela's coast, the death toll has passed 4,300 with nearly 17,000 injured (The Guardian). The disaster is now reshaping politics as much as geography: The Guardian reports fury among residents of a flattened government housing project, with mounting anger at Chavez's political heirs over a slow, underprepared response, while the NYT reports Venezuelans "openly and forcefully voicing their anger" for the first time in years — even as a youth baseball league mourns players killed in the collapse. Three weeks on, the story has shifted from search-and-rescue to reconstruction, and from grief to blame — a reminder that geology can redraw a coastline in seconds, but the political map takes longer to settle.

**The historical parallel**

This week's Polish-Ukrainian dispute over WWII-era killings is really an argument about a border that no longer exists. Our library's "Border Changes in History of Poland" map overlays four moments in Polish history on the same outline: Boleslaw I's kingdom around the year 1000, the vast Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth of 1596, the interwar Second Polish Republic of 1939, and the People's Republic of 1945. The 1939 borders reached deep into what is now western Ukraine, including the Volhynia region where the massacres Poland wants memorialized took place. After 1945, the Soviet Union pushed Poland's entire territory roughly 200km west — handing Volhynia to Ukraine and compensating Poland with former German land in the west. The killings happened on Polish soil; eighty years later, the argument over them continues on Ukrainian soil.
